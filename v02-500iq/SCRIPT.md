# ★2 · "What If You Had 500 IQ in the Stone Age?"

**Format:** long-form 16:9 (YouTube watch hours) · ~7:30 · plus a 60 s vertical cut for Shorts and Reels
**Borrowed formula:** Ifverse "What If You Had 500 IQ?" (1.4M) plus its sequel series, moved 50,000 years back
**The payoff (real science):** a genius alone can't out-think the Stone Age, because human progress comes from groups sharing knowledge, the "collective brain". That's a twist people will argue about in the comments.

## Script (narration, second person; ~1,050 words)

**[0:00] HOOK**
You wake up 50,000 years ago… with an IQ of 500. You know calculus. You know germs exist. You know exactly how a steam engine works. You should be able to rule this world by Friday.
By the end of this video, you'll find out why you'd probably be dead by Thursday, and the strange reason the smartest person alive back then wasn't really a person at all.

**[0:25] DAY 1: THE PLAN**
Day one. You stand at the edge of a camp. Twenty-five people, three fires, zero patience for strangers. Your plan is simple: invent everything. Fire, the wheel, medicine, maybe a small empire.
First problem: fire. They already have it. Humans have been using fire for hundreds of thousands of years. The kid next to you makes one in under a minute. You know the chemistry of combustion. He knows which wood actually burns.

**[1:10] THE BRAIN TWIST**
Here's what surprises most people. These aren't dim cavemen. People 50,000 years ago had brains basically like yours. Same size, same capacity. If one of their babies were raised today, it could become a programmer, a pilot, a doctor.
So your 500 IQ isn't facing a world of idiots. It's facing a world of geniuses with no notebooks.

**[1:45] DAY 3: THE WHEEL**
Day three. You decide to invent the wheel. Easy, right?
Except a wheel needs an axle. An axle needs precise carving. Carving needs metal tools. Metal needs mining, smelting, and furnaces. Furnaces need clay, fuel and generations of trial and error. Your wheel needs about forty thousand years of other inventions first.
You end up with a nice round piece of wood, and nowhere to roll it. No roads, just mud, rocks and forest.

**[2:40] DAY 7: MEDICINE**
Day seven. Someone cuts their leg. You know about germs, so you yell: "Boil the water!"
In what? Pots are tens of thousands of years in the future; the oldest known pottery is about twenty thousand years old. You try heating stones and dropping them into a skin bag of water. It works. Slowly. Everyone looks at you like you just discovered water is wet.
It's your first real win. Remember it, because it's the clue to the whole video.

**[3:25] DAY 20: THE HUNT**
Day twenty. Food is running low. You design the perfect weapon: a bow. You calculate draw weight, arrow length, fletching angles.
Plot twist: someone may have beaten you to it. Researchers studying a cave in France found tiny stone points that look like arrowheads, around fifty-four thousand years old. If they're right, the bow already exists, invented by people with no math at all, just countless shots, misses and small improvements passed down.

**[4:15] DAY 60: THE COLLAPSE**
Day sixty. Winter hits. You know exactly why winter happens: Earth's axial tilt. That fact keeps you exactly zero degrees warmer.
Meanwhile, an old woman in the camp knows which plants survive the frost, where the reindeer cross the river, and how to sew a hide tight enough to keep wind out. She can't read. She's never heard of Newton. And she's keeping everyone alive, including you.

**[5:00] THE TRUTH: THE COLLECTIVE BRAIN**
And this is the twist. The smartest thing in this camp isn't you. It's the group.
Scientists have a name for it: the collective brain. Human progress doesn't come from one genius inventing everything. It comes from lots of people copying, sharing and slightly improving what came before, across generations.
There's even a famous, and debated, example. When rising seas cut Tasmania off from mainland Australia, some researchers argue its small population slowly lost skills over thousands of years, like making certain fishing and bone tools. Fewer people meant fewer minds to keep knowledge alive.
So what does 500 IQ actually do in the Stone Age? On your own, almost nothing. Inside a group, it becomes the most valuable thing on Earth.

**[6:10] DAY 365: WHAT YOU ACTUALLY CHANGE**
So you stop trying to invent everything. You start teaching. You show the kids how to count in tens with their fingers. You show the hunters a better way to tie a spear point. You teach everyone to boil water before drinking it. Small things. Copyable things.
And here's the strange part. None of them will remember your name. But the ideas spread from camp to camp, generation to generation. A bit like this video spreading from person to person, and that's how anything survives.

**[6:55] ENDING + QUESTION**
So what if you had 500 IQ in the Stone Age? You wouldn't rule the world. But you might help start it a little sooner.
Now be honest: what's the first thing YOU would teach them? Fire safety, counting, medicine… or something nobody has thought of yet? Tell me below. The best answer might become the next video.

## Comment fuel and subconscious CTA
- **"Brains basically like yours"** is counter-intuitive; people will debate it.
- **Tasmania (debated)** gives the experts something to argue about.
- **"What's the first thing YOU would teach them?"** gives an open answer plus status: "the best answer becomes the next video".
- **Subconscious share and follow cue:** "ideas spread… a bit like this video spreading from person to person". It makes sharing feel like part of the story's lesson.

## Sources to verify before publishing
- Modern brain capacity ~50,000 years ago: e.g. Neubauer et al. 2018, *Science Advances* (brain shape reached modern range 100,000–35,000 years ago). The narration keeps it as "basically like yours".
- Fire use: hundreds of thousands of years (multiple sites).
- Oldest pottery ~20,000 years: Xianrendong Cave, China (Wu et al. 2012, *Science*).
- Possible arrowheads ~54,000 years: Grotte Mandrin, France (Metz et al. 2023, *Science Advances*). The narration says "may have", "if they're right".
- Collective brain and Tasmania: Henrich 2004 (*American Antiquity*). The narration labels it as debated.
- Axial tilt causes seasons: established.
