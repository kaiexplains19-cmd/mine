# What if You Woke Up 50,000 Years Ago?

**Runtime target:** about 7:30 · **Narration:** about 1,075 words (about 6:55 at 155 wpm) plus about 35 s of silent gags and music beats · **POV:** second person
*Section timestamps are planning targets. Re-time the chapters from the final edit.*
**Recurring characters:** YOU (orange hoodie, phone), **Ama** (the band's elder, calm and unimpressed), **Tik** (a kid who follows you everywhere and copies everything you do), **Brug** (a Neanderthal who is better at everything than you), **the phone** (its battery % is a running gag)

`[brackets]` = visuals and SFX · **BADGE** = on-screen year/day counter · `>>` = character line

---

## 0:00 · Cold open

`[Bedroom at night. Phone on the pillow: 11:52 PM, battery 14%.]`

It's 11:52 p.m. You plug in your phone. It doesn't charge. You'll deal with it tomorrow.

`[Lights out. SFX: wind. Cut to black, then an enormous starfield.]`

You open your eyes. No ceiling. No streetlights. A sky with so many stars it looks like a mistake.

And a smell. Smoke, and something that has never showered. Ever.

`[A crackling fire. Three round black faces lean into frame, staring at you.]`

>> **YOU:** …Hi?

`[Phone screen: "No Service. Searching…"]`

You've woken up 50,000 years ago.

No shops. No doctors. Nobody who speaks your language. And the next person you know won't be born for about fifty thousand years.

Your phone is searching for signal. It'll be searching for a while.

## 0:28 · Title card

`[Music hit. TITLE: "WHAT IF YOU WOKE UP 50,000 YEARS AGO?" carved into a rock face.]`

## 0:33 · Act 1: the world you woke up in
**BADGE: DAY 1**

First, where are you? Not in a world of "cavemen". In a world of humans. Several kinds.

`[Map of Africa and Eurasia. Four silhouettes pop up in different regions.]`

Right now, at least four kinds of humans walk the Earth. Your kind, Homo sapiens, has been spreading out of Africa. Neanderthals live across Europe and western Asia. Denisovans are somewhere in Asia. And on one Indonesian island, there may still be tiny humans about a metre tall.

The animals are bigger than you remember, too.

`[Woolly mammoth walks past the fire. Scale bar: a bus.]`

A full-grown woolly mammoth can weigh about six tonnes. There are cave lions, cave hyenas, and bears the size of a small car.

`[Breath fog. You hug yourself in your hoodie.]`

And it's cold. The world is in the middle of an ice age. Huge sheets of ice cover the far north, and so much of the world's water is locked up in ice that the seas are tens of metres lower than today. Your hoodie is rated for "chilly evening". Not "ice age".

The good news? No rent. No emails. Clean air, and rivers full of fish. You've watched survival shows. How hard can it be?

`[Smug face. SFX: record scratch.]`

Easy.

## 1:20 · The turn

Except you can't make fire.

`[Montage: you rubbing two sticks. Time counter speeds up: 5 min… 20 min… 40 min.]`

Forty minutes later you have a blister and a slightly warm stick. Tik is watching, very confused.

>> **TIK:** `[makes fire in about ten seconds with flint, and hands it to you]`

You don't know which berries are food and which are a funeral. You don't know which river water is safe. And at night, the dark isn't empty.

`[Glowing eyes appear in the treeline, one pair at a time.]`

But the biggest danger isn't the lions. It's being alone.

Hunter-gatherer bands were usually a few dozen people who shared everything: food, fire, babies, and watching for danger. Someone on their own in this world wouldn't last long.

So you need a tribe. And luckily, one just found you.

## 2:05 · Act 2, scene 1: the job interview

`[Ama circles you slowly. The band stares at your hoodie.]`

Ama, the oldest woman in the band, wants to know one thing: what are you good for?

`[Skill checklist on bark: TRACK ✗ HUNT ✗ FIRE ✗ PLANTS ✗]`

You can't track. You can't hunt. You can't tell a good mushroom from a bad one.

>> **YOU:** I can… explain cryptocurrency?

`[Ama walks away.]`

You get the job no one wants: carrying things. And every day, you walk.

**BADGE: WEEK 1**

Today's Hadza hunter-gatherers in Tanzania walk somewhere around 8 to 12 kilometres a day. You'd think that burns a mountain of calories. It doesn't. When scientists measured it, the Hadza burned about the same energy per day as people in offices, once body size was taken into account. Your body is cheap. It adjusts.

`[You, sweaty, looking down at your stomach.]`

So you won't get abs. You'll just be tired.

`[Shoes fall apart. Tik puts one on his head.]`

Dinner is whatever the day found: roots, nuts, fish, the occasional something with too many legs. `[Tik offers you a grub. You eat it. Long pause.]` Tastes like regret. With a hint of almond.

`[Night. The band asleep in a pile around the fire. You, wide awake, staring at the stars.]`

At least you'll sleep well out here, right? No screens, no traffic, no 2 a.m. scrolling.

Not exactly. When scientists tracked sleep in three hunter-gatherer and farming groups who live without electricity, they slept about six to seven hours a night. About the same as you.

They just don't wake up and check their phone.

`[You reach for your phone. 9%. You put it back. Tik, eyes closed, puts it back further.]`

And somebody is always awake. In a camp, there's almost always someone up: tending the fire, listening for the night.

`[Ama's silhouette by the fire, watching the treeline.]`

Tonight, that someone is Ama. She's been doing this for forty years. Nothing gets past her. Not even you.

## 3:05 · Act 2, scene 2: the neighbours

`[Morning fog. A stocky figure with a heavy brow steps out of the mist. Big nose, strong shoulders. The band freezes.]`

Then one morning, you meet someone who isn't quite… you.

Meet Brug. Brug is a Neanderthal.

Forget the old cartoon of a dumb brute. Neanderthals made tools, controlled fire, looked after their sick, and on average had brains at least as big as ours.

`[Brug builds a perfect fire in seconds. He looks at your blister. Pats your head.]`

Brug is better than you at everything.

And here's the part the old textbooks used to skip. Around this time, our ancestors and Neanderthals were having children together.

`[Brug and a sapiens woman sharing food, heart-eyes. The band is scandalised. Tik gives a thumbs up.]`

Genetic studies suggest most of the mixing happened in one long stretch, roughly 50,500 to 43,500 years ago. Right when you've arrived.

If your family comes from outside Africa, around 1 to 2% of your DNA probably comes from someone like Brug.

`[You look at Brug. Brug looks at you.]`

So Brug might be your great-great-great… a few thousand greats… grandpa.

You decide not to tell him.

## 4:05 · Time-skip: the first winter
**BADGE: FIRST WINTER** `[Colour drains to blue-grey. Snow. The band huddles around one fire.]`

Then winter comes. And this is where the Stone Age stops being funny.

`[Phone: 2%. You scroll to one photo: your mum. The screen goes black.]`

The phone finally dies. The last thing you saw on it was your mum.

Everyone you've ever loved won't exist for fifty thousand years. You can't call them. You can't even tell anyone about them.

`[Ama sits next to you. Wordlessly puts a fur around your shoulders.]`

Life here is hard in ways that are difficult to picture. In hunter-gatherer groups studied by anthropologists, only a little over half of children lived to fifteen.

`[Tik, asleep against your arm.]`

But something else is true too. Some of the people you love back home might be descended from the people sleeping next to you right now.

## 4:55 · Hope: the first storytellers
**BADGE: SPRING**

`[A cave by torchlight. A woman blows red pigment around her hand onto the rock.]`

Then spring comes, and you learn something nobody ever told you: these people are artists.

On the Indonesian island of Sulawesi, someone painted three human-like figures next to a wild pig on a cave wall. Scientists dated it to at least 51,200 years ago. It's one of the oldest pictures of a story ever found.

`[You and Tik in the cave. You dip your finger in ochre.]`

So you tell your story the only way you can.

`[You paint a rectangle. A cracked screen. A tiny "2%".]`

## 5:25 · Time-skip montage

**BADGE: YEAR 1** · You can finally make fire. Tik slow-claps.

**BADGE: YEAR 3** · You bring down your first deer. It was mostly Brug.

**BADGE: YEAR 10** · You're teaching kids which berries are a funeral.

`[Tik, now tall, carrying YOU over a river.]`

**BADGE: YEAR 20** · You're the old one now.

Here's something surprising. The average life expectancy in groups like these was low, but mostly because so many children died. Adults who made it to their mid-forties often lived almost another twenty years.

`[You, grey-bearded, by the fire, telling a story with big hand gestures. Kids laughing.]`

>> **YOU:** …and it fit in your pocket, and it knew everything.

>> **TIK (adult):** Sure it did.

## 6:30 · The answer

So, could you survive if you woke up 50,000 years ago?

Alone? Probably not past the first week.

With a band? Maybe. And you'd find out the Stone Age wasn't full of "cavemen". It was full of people. Same brain as you. Same jokes. Same fear of the dark. Just no Wi-Fi.

`[Pull back: the fire, the band, the stars. Text chips float up: "FIRST FARMS: 38,000 YEARS FROM NOW" · "FIRST WRITING: 45,000 YEARS FROM NOW"]`

The first farms are still 38,000 years away. Writing, about 45,000.

Everything you think of as "history" hasn't happened yet. And the people sitting around this fire are why it eventually does.

## 7:15 · Punchline and comment question

**BADGE: 50,000 YEARS LATER**

`[Present day. An archaeologist in a cave, shining a torch on a faded red rectangle with a crack and "2%".]`

>> **ARCHAEOLOGIST:** Fascinating. Probably a ritual object.

`[Smash cut to black.]`

If you woke up 50,000 years ago and could bring **one** thing from today, what would it be?

Not your phone. Trust me. It dies.

`[End screen: "NEXT: What if You Were the Last Neanderthal?"]`
